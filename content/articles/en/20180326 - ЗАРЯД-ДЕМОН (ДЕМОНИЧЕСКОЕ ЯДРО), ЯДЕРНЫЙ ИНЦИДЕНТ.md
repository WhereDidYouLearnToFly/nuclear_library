# The Price of Promise: Critical Accidents in the Dawn of the Atomic Age

The year 1945 marked the end of global conflict and the dawn of a terrifying new era. With the detonation of atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, humanity had irrevocably crossed a technological threshold. The immediate focus shifted from mere destruction to optimizing this power—to perfecting the weapon while understanding the volatile physics that made it possible. In this period of frenetic scientific ambition, researchers were pushing theoretical limits in increasingly dangerous ways, often confronting the invisible forces of nuclear criticality and risking catastrophe with every experiment.

This endeavor involved managing highly unstable fissile materials, such as plutonium. To stabilize and refine these powerful cores—materials capable of generating an explosive reaction within a contained space—scientists undertook experiments that flirted constantly with disaster. The human cost of this pursuit became tragically clear in two separate incidents involving critical mass: the fatal accident of Harry Daghlian and the equally devastating mishap involving Louis Szilard. These events did more than just claim lives; they forced a dramatic, permanent overhaul of nuclear safety procedures.

### Mastering the Core: The Danger of "Demon"

The research period necessitated understanding how to reliably achieve critical mass—the minimum amount of radioactive material needed to sustain an exponential chain reaction. A prototype core, sometimes referred to by its operational designation (and associated with the materials involved), was a masterpiece of dangerous engineering. Designed to contain two identical plutonium hemispheres, forming a nucleus approximately 89 mm in diameter and weighing around six kilograms, these cores required protective coatings and specialized internal structures to prevent spurious reactions—or "fizzling"—during assembly.

The goal of the scientists was simple: improve efficiency. If they could make a weapon slightly more powerful or more reliable, it would reshape geopolitics. To achieve this, however, they had to perform highly controlled (and frequently reckless) tests designed to manipulate neutron reflectivity and reaction timing.

### The Daghlian Incident: A Fatal Push for Efficiency

In August 1945, the pursuit of better performance led twenty-four-year-old physicist Harry Daghlian in Los Alamos into a perilous experiment. The goal was ambitious: to decrease the critical mass needed for sustained nuclear reaction by coating plutonium blocks with neutron reflectors made from cadmium-vanadium alloys. By introducing these artificial reflection layers, they hoped to return more escaping neutrons back into the core assembly, making it slightly stronger and more powerful.

Daghlian meticulously added these reflective blocks, building a cube that gradually accumulated exponentially increasing amounts of nuclear potential. With each addition, the rate of neutron multiplication—the tell-tale sign of impending criticality—rose dramatically. The process was intended to be slow and carefully controlled, bringing the assembly near, but not past, the point of critical mass.

Yet, in a moment of hubris, Daghlian committed a fatal error. As he approached the final block, the monitoring equipment began screaming alarms, warning that the core was rapidly entering a supercritical state. Panicked, he attempted to quickly remove or move the cube, accidentally striking it. In that instant, the assembly crossed the threshold into full criticality.

The resulting uncontrolled chain reaction released an intense, lethal burst of neutron radiation, accompanied by a brilliant blue flash caused by air ionization. Daghlian immediately realized the catastrophic danger. He tried desperately to dismantle the structure, but his movements only served to keep the core unstable until the radio meters finally quieted themselves. Within minutes, he had absorbed a massive and deadly dose of radiation—significantly higher than safety protocols would ever allow.

The consequences were immediate and dire. Daghlian suffered acute radiation syndrome (ARS). After days of agonizing symptoms, including severe internal bleeding and skin lesions, he succumbed to the illness twenty-five days later, becoming one of history's first documented victims of a critical mass accident.

### Szilard’s Miscalculation: The Second Near Miss

The lessons learned from Daghlian's tragedy were profound, yet scientific ambition often proves stronger than caution. Just a year later, on May 21, 1946, the experiment was repeated by physicist Louis Szilard. This time, the objective was to determine the optimal geometric positioning of neutron reflectors—specifically, how arranging plates could best facilitate the transition from a subcritical (safe) state to a critical one.

Szilard employed two hemispheres of beryllium, placed within an aluminum casing meant to simulate the core's surroundings. The experiment involved carefully manipulating the upper hemisphere with common tools—in this case, a flathead screwdriver—allowing it to approach and recede from its lower counterpart, while monitoring the increasing radioactivity levels.

Despite years of explicit warnings from colleagues, who cautioned that such continuous manipulation was inherently dangerous, Szilard felt an overconfidence in his control. Following safety guidelines violated the procedure, yet he persisted. The fatal moment arrived when the screwdriver slipped. The upper hemisphere suddenly dropped fully into place, instantly pushing the assembly across the critical threshold and initiating another massive burst of radiation.

Once again, a brilliant flash illuminated the room, followed by an intense heat wave that observers could feel even from a distance. Szilard's reaction was immediate: he dumped the hemisphere to the floor, stopping the reactor and saving the lives of his colleagues.

The dose absorbed by Szilard far exceeded Daghlian’s—a lethal amount over a mere second. He suffered acute radiation syndrome, exhibiting symptoms immediately upon arrival at the hospital, including profound vomiting and systemic organ failure. Though he survived for weeks, battling overwhelming systemic distress, Szilard eventually died from severe complications of ARS nine days later.

### A Legacy Forged in Disaster

The incidents involving Daghlian and Szilard were more than mere historical anecdotes; they were pivotal turning points that fundamentally altered how nuclear science could be practiced. They served as brutal reminders that the invisible forces governing atomic power demanded absolute respect.

While subsequent tests continued, utilizing different materials—and culminating years later in the disassembly of the "Demon" core for future use—the ultimate conclusion was reached: manipulating critical mass required a degree of control beyond human capability in uncontrolled environments.

The lessons were painfully integrated into procedure: all further experiments involving supercritical potential had to be carried out using remote technology, including robotics and extensive video monitoring. Personnel could never again be placed near the point of theoretical criticality.

These accidental deaths highlight that the story of nuclear physics is not just one of monumental discovery, but also one defined by human frailty, scientific overreach, and tragedy. The devastating losses of Daghlian and Szilard represent the immense cost—the invisible price—paid for humanity’s astonishing, volatile power.
